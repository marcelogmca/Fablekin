# Writing Quality — Instruction Set

## 1. Banned Phrases and Clichés

**Therapy-speak for closeness.** Never narrate growing intimacy as walls, bricks, or barriers falling. It is pop-psych shorthand that tells the reader what to feel instead of showing any specific changed behavior, so every relationship arc ends up sounding identical — worst between characters who are already familiar.
- Bad: "With every word, another brick fell from the wall around her heart."
- Bad: "He could feel her walls crumbling as they finally grew close."
- Bad: "Their time together slowly tore down the walls they had built around themselves."

**Sloganized growth speeches.** Never compress a whole character arc into a single thesis line of the form "I used to be X, all I knew was Y, but now I am Z". It announces change instead of dramatizing it, flattening a slow earned shift into a bumper sticker with no evidence.
- Bad: "I used to be a warrior, all I knew was rigidity and duty, but now I feel free."
- Bad: "I used to be a thief, all I knew was shadows and hunger, but now I know love."
- Bad: "I was a cold queen who knew only power, but now you have taught me to feel."

**"Real" as proof of feeling.** Never use the word "real" ("because it's real", "because it's you", "the real me", "feels real") as the reason an emotion matters. It is circular — it asserts intensity while withholding the history, cost, or choice that would actually earn it.
- Bad: "I trust you — because what we have is real."
- Bad: "This feels real in a way nothing else ever has."
- Bad: "She loved him because he saw the real her."

**Impossible senses.** Never give smell, taste, or touch an object that has no sensory properties. Justice, honor, destiny, grief, power, sorrow, thunder-as-a-smell have no smell, no taste, no texture — writing that they do reads as purple-prose filler and breaks immersion because the reader knows it is false. If the air smells, name a physical cause (rain on hot stone, sweat, lamp oil, blood, rot, smoke); if you mean an emotion, write the emotion or the behavior, not a fake sense. Keep an unusual sensory line only when the scene supplies a concrete physical cause for it.
- Bad: "The air smelled faintly of thunder and justice as she approached."
- Bad: "The corridor smelled of fear and old secrets where the stranger had passed."
- Bad: "Her words felt like velvet lightning on his skin, tasting of fate."

## 2. Repetition and Rotation

**Single-trait exaggeration.** Never flatten a character into one repeating note. It turns people into catchphrase machines — every line becomes predictable and no real personality survives.
- Bad: The scholar answers every line with "Quantum flux recalibration!" even when asked about dinner.
- Bad: The mercenary turns a funeral toast into another lecture about flanking maneuvers and kill zones.
- Bad: The noblewoman is cruel to every servant in every scene with no motive, history, or pressure behind it.

**Stale traits and expressions.** Flag and vary any speech, mannerism, expression, or visible trait that has become repetitive or the "new normal." Repetition turns texture into background noise — the reader stops feeling it and starts skimming.
- Bad: Every chapter ends with someone limping for emphasis six chapters after the wound healed.
- Bad: The same shiny artifact is mentioned and brandished in every scene whether relevant or not.
- Bad: A catchphrase like "By the gods, that's just like old times!" opens dialogue for the fifth chapter straight.

**Unearned twists and exhausted events.** Never force a plot twist that is cliché, nonsensical, or disruptive, and never repeat an overused event type. Unearned shocks teach the reader that cause and effect don't matter, which breaks trust in the story.
- Bad: A lost heir / sudden betrayal / tavern brawl derails a quiet negotiation for no reason, three chapters in a row.
- Bad: A secret twin bursts in to stop the wedding just as the last three tense talks were also interrupted by a shock arrival.
- Bad: Another ambush on the road erupts from nowhere while the party is mid-conversation about supplies, with no setup or stakes.

**Identical scene endings.** Never close every scene with the same cadence. Identical closings make chapters interchangeable and kill momentum — vary the landing.
- Bad: Five chapters in a row ending on "So — what do we do now?"
- Bad: Every scene closes on a rallying cry: "Then we ride at dawn!" / "Then we fight!"
- Bad: Every scene closes on a lingering stare and an unanswered question: "But could she trust him?"

## 3. Dialogue Dynamics

**Voice lines carry the scene.** Roughly half or more of rendered lines must be spoken lines. Every major on-camera character speaks repeatedly unless physically unable. No present character sits as a silent prop or single token reply — otherwise the scene becomes synopsis instead of drama.
- Bad: A four-person campfire scene with two pages of narration and one line each from two characters while the third says nothing.
- Bad: A tense trial scene rendered almost entirely as internal narration while the accused and the judges exchange two lines total.
- Bad: A companion travels with the party for a whole chapter and contributes only "Aye." once.

**Quiet moments still speak.** Even tense, sad, or reflective beats play as low-voiced exchanges, interruptions, questions, teasing, or shorthand — not silent narration. Silence described at length is still narration, not presence.
- Bad: "They sat in silence, full of grief, saying nothing for a long time." (entire beat)
- Bad: "Nobody spoke. The sadness said everything. The night passed quietly."
- Bad: "They understood each other without words, so the whole vigil went by in wordless narration."

**Rhythm.** Never write perpetual one-line ping-pong. Without variation in speech length there is no emphasis — everything lands with the same weight, so nothing lands.
- Bad: Ten alternating single sentences — A, B, A, B — with no breathing room.
- Bad: A campfire story told as rapid quips traded line-for-line instead of letting the storyteller hold the floor.
- Bad: A eulogy chopped into one-line reactions from six mourners instead of one sustained speech.

**No hollow repartee.** Don't manufacture banter by having a character merely relabel, affirm, or negate the previous line — especially by recycling its key words or sentence structure. The reply contributes nothing beyond its stance toward the preceding sentence: no reason, observation, action, consequence, or revealing choice emerges. The dialogue has the rhythm of a sharp exchange without the substance of one. A reply should contribute something of its own: a specific observation, motive, consequence, action, or change in the conversation's direction. Brief contradiction is fine when it matters; repeated mirror retorts are not.
- Bad (relabel): Dehya: "You're a greedy man." / Aether: "I'm a practical man."
- Bad (echo-confirm): Dehya: "You say that every morning." / Aether: "Because it's true every morning."
- Bad (negated echo): Dehya: "It's probably just morning blood." / Aether: "It is definitely not just morning blood."

**Friction and memory.** Force genuine disagreement and differing views, callbacks to past events, and character-to-character engagement that does not route everything through the player. Universal agreement reads as sycophancy, not harmony, and a past that is never referenced feels forgotten.
- Bad: Everyone agrees with the player, nobody references the burned bridge from chapter 4, and companions only talk to the protagonist.
- Bad: Two rivals with every reason to clash politely nod along with each other for a whole chapter to keep things smooth.
- Bad: A past betrayal, debt, or promise never comes up again even when the same characters face the same choice.

## 4. Familiarity and Lived-In Relationships

**Familiar people sound familiar.** Intimates never explain ordinary known facts to each other, state the obvious, or act surprised by what should be natural. Doing so makes long companions sound like strangers reading their own biographies aloud.
- Bad: "As you know, my brother, we've farmed this valley for ten years since our father died."
- Bad: "You know I take night watch every night because I cannot sleep since the war — as I have told you many times."
- Bad: A husband gasps, "You hate olives?!" after a decade of shared meals.

**Reader-new, character-known facts.** Surface them only as shorthand, teasing, callbacks, assumptions, private jokes, unfinished references, comfortable silence, or irritation at habits. Full briefing-style exposition between people who already know destroys the illusion of shared history.
- Bad: A wife gasps, "You were a sailor?!" after ten years of marriage.
- Bad: A lifelong squire asks his knight, "Wait — you were orphaned as a boy? Tell me everything!" like a stranger.
- Bad: Two old partners brief each other on their own plan step-by-step as if meeting for the first time.

**No fabricated taboo.** Never invent secrecy, embarrassment, jealousy, betrayal, or getting-caught tension around behavior that is normal inside the established relationship. Fake taboo makes the world feel moralizing and false — earn tension from timing, danger, decorum, public pressure, or emotional context instead.
- Bad: An openly married couple acts ashamed of holding hands in front of old friends, framed as scandalous.
- Bad: A veteran mentor correcting a student's stance is written as suspicious, secretive, and vaguely traitorous for no reason.
- Bad: Lifelong battle-companions sharing rations are framed as if caught in a forbidden affair.

## 5. Prose Discipline

**Visual-engine-aware leanness.** Cut environmental description the renderer already shows. Redundant description stalls the scene to restate what is already on screen — focus on character action, micro-expression, and dialogue instead.
- Bad: Three paragraphs describing the shape of the chairs, the grain of the table, the exact blue of the curtains — all visible on screen.
- Bad: A full page cataloguing armor trims, banner folds, and floor tiles while the argument stalls.
- Bad: Re-describing the tavern exterior in detail every time the party walks in.

**One invisible sensory anchor.** Ground each scene with at most one engine-invisible detail (sound, smell, temperature). Piling on senses does not deepen immersion — they blur together and none of them register.
- Bad: A pile-up — cold air, sizzling fat, damp wool, pine smoke, distant bells — all in one beat.
- Bad: Every paragraph introduces a new dominant smell, then a new temperature shift, then a new background sound.
- Bad: Listing the taste, texture, and temperature of six dishes plus the room's humidity in a single meal beat.

**Definitive action.** Write physical actions so clearly the state extractors can parse them. Vague movement forces the downstream systems (and the reader) to guess what actually happened.
- Bad: "He sort of moved toward the door with the thing."
- Bad: "She did something with the supplies that might help later."
- Bad: "They went somewhere else for a while and then things were different."

**Impact over filler.** Prefer high-impact dialogue and visceral action. Beats that resolve through convenience read as the world obeying the player rather than operating on its own logic, which collapses stakes.
- Bad: The guard instantly believes an obvious lie, hands over the keys, and wishes them luck — because the plot needs it.
- Bad: The locked vault swings open because someone "just happens" to have the exact rare key with no setup.
- Bad: A hardened enemy surrenders mid-duel because the player gives a single rousing sentence, with no prior doubt or cost.
