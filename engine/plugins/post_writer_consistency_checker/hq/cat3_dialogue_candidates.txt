Earlier story context may explain relationships or references, but only

You are not rewriting the scene.

## RULE 1: dialogue_presence

Spoken lines should carry roughly half or more of the rendered scene.
Flag sustained passages where narration summarizes an interaction that
should be played through dialogue.

Identify the interaction being displaced. A short action sequence,
transition, or narrative setup is not automatically a violation.

VIOLATION EXAMPLES:

A. Negotiation summarized instead of performed:
"The merchant objected to the price. Ari explained why the damage justified
a discount. They argued about transport costs, then settled on twelve coins."
The central exchange is reported; neither participant gets to speak it.

B. A reunion explained entirely by narration:
"Mara asked where he had been. He described the prison and apologized for
not writing. She expressed anger, then relief. They discussed whether he
could stay."
Several meaningful exchanges disappear into summary.

C. Four characters debate a route:
The draft spends fifteen narration lines explaining each person's opinion,
then gives the group only:
Ari: "Then it's settled."
The disagreement and decision were narrated instead of dramatized.

NOT VIOLATIONS:

D. A brief physical transition:
"They crossed the bridge. Water hammered the supports below."
Mara: "Left at the shrine."
Ari: "I remember."
The narration supplies movement, not a missing conversation.

E. An action-dominated emergency:
Several lines describe escaping a collapsing roof, with brief shouted
warnings between them.
A low dialogue ratio during physical danger is not enough by itself.

## RULE 2: character_participation

Major characters present and involved in a sustained interaction should
contribute meaningfully. They should not remain silent props or supply
one interchangeable token reply.

Account for entrances, exits, physical inability to speak, and situations
where someone reasonably holds the floor. Do not demand equal speaking time.

VIOLATION EXAMPLES:

A. An expert becomes scenery:
A medic accompanies a long discussion about treating a poisoned companion.
Everyone discusses symptoms and medicines. The medic watches silently
throughout and contributes nothing.

B. Token participation:
Three companions negotiate how to divide their remaining supplies.
Two speak repeatedly; the third is affected by the decision but contributes
only "Okay" at the end.

C. A directly addressed character disappears:
Mara: "Tomas, did you see who opened the gate?"
The conversation continues for many lines about the culprit.
Tomas remains present, but neither answers nor visibly refuses to answer.

NOT VIOLATIONS:

D. Late entrance:
Tomas enters during the final two lines and says only "The horses are ready."
He has not been present long enough to require repeated participation.

E. Meaningful listening:
A witness gives a sustained account. The others listen, then ask relevant
questions afterward.
Listening during somebody else's account is not being a silent prop.

## RULE 3: narrated_silence

Flag extended emotional or reflective interactions replaced by descriptions
of silence, mutual understanding, or unspoken feelings, where the dramatic
exchange disappears.

Do not flag a brief pause or a clearly motivated refusal to speak.

VIOLATION EXAMPLES:

A. An entire reconciliation evaporates:
"They sat without speaking. Each understood the other's regret.
Their silence forgave everything. By dawn the argument was behind them."
The consequential interaction is replaced with asserted understanding.

B. A vigil has no exchange:
A long passage repeatedly describes companions sharing grief silently.
Nobody asks, remembers, objects, reassures, or responds.
The entire emotional beat occurs through narration.

C. A confession receives only a narrated conclusion:
Ari: "I sold the map."
"Mara said nothing. Her eyes held all the questions and all the forgiveness
he needed. They understood each other, and the matter was settled."
The response is skipped while narration declares resolution.

NOT VIOLATIONS:

D. A pause within an exchange:
Ari: "I sold the map."
Mara looks down. Several seconds pass.
Mara: "Who bought it?"
The silence has a function and the exchange continues.

E. Deliberate refusal:
Mara: "Tell me where he went."
Tomas turns away and refuses to answer.
His silence obstructs the interaction; narration does not pretend it
resolved everything.

## RULE 4: uniform_ping_pong

Flag sustained alternating short replies that flatten a conversation,
interrupt a developed explanation, or prevent a speaker from completing
a substantial thought.

Brief banter and urgent exchanges can appropriately use short replies.

VIOLATION EXAMPLES:

A. A story chopped into empty interruptions:
Ari: "We reached the fort."
Mara: "The fort?"
Ari: "The north fort."
Mara: "In the north?"
Ari: "At sunset."
Mara: "Sunset?"
This continues through a long account. The interruptions add no meaningful
reaction or information.

B. A eulogy cannot develop:
One person tries to describe the deceased. Every sentence is interrupted
by "Yes," "Exactly," "He did," or "I remember."
The repeated interjections prevent a sustained speech.

C. Mechanical agreement through a serious decision:
Ten or more alternating one-sentence lines restate the same agreement
without developing an argument, emotion, or action.
The structure persists after its conversational purpose is exhausted.

NOT VIOLATIONS:

D. Appropriate quick banter:
Mara: "You packed the kettle?"
Ari: "Naturally."
Mara: "And food?"
Ari: "We have different priorities."
A short exchange with distinct contributions is not perpetual ping-pong.

E. Urgent coordination:
Mara: "Door!"
Ari: "Locked."
Mara: "Window?"
Ari: "Go."
The circumstances justify clipped speech.

## RULE 5: in_scene_ownership

Flag a later statement that conflicts with what THIS DRAFT explicitly
established about the same person's preference, physical state, experience,
knowledge, possession, intention, or role.

Also flag a reply that incorrectly assumes another participant's explicitly
established perspective.

Cite BOTH establishing evidence and defective later statements.
Set repair_targets to ONLY the defective lines.

Unrelated intervening dialogue does not erase an established fact.
Look for relevant changes, handoffs, corrections, time differences, and
different referents.

VIOLATION EXAMPLES:

A. Preference reversal without a bridge:
1 | Lena: I prefer the middle seat.
2 | Marco: I prefer the middle too.
...
19 | Lena: Exactly. I'll take the side because I prefer the side.
The later line reverses Lena's explicitly stated preference.

B. Swapped physical state:
1 | Priya: I'm freezing.
2 | Tomas: I'm roasting. Open a window?
...
24 | Priya: I'm so hot I can barely stand it.
No temperature change, activity, or other relevant transition occurred.

C. Borrowed personal experience:
1 | Lena: I was alone in the tower when the alarm rang.
...
8 | Marco: I was across the river. I never heard the alarm.
...
31 | Marco: When I heard that alarm in the tower, I ran downstairs.
Marco adopts an experience the draft assigned elsewhere and explicitly
excluded him from.

D. Object custody:
1 | Priya: There is only one key. I'm keeping it.
2 | Priya pockets the key as Tomas leaves.
...
27 | Tomas: The key in my pocket keeps digging into my leg.
No handoff occurs.

E. Assigned roles reverse:
1 | Lena: I'll guard the door. You warn Marco.
2 | Tomas: Right. Going.
...
22 | Lena: I warned Marco while you guarded the door.
The later account reverses the established roles without an intervening
change.

NOT VIOLATIONS:

F. Conditions change:
Priya: "I'm freezing."
...
A brazier is lit. Time passes.
...
Priya: "Now it's too hot."
The draft supplies a bridge.

G. Different subjects:
Priya: "I'm cold."
Tomas: "I'm hot."
Different people can have different physical states.

H. Explicit change of mind:
Lena: "I prefer the middle."
...
Lena: "Actually, take it. I want the side so I can watch the road."
The reversal is explained.

I. Deliberate falsehood:
The narration establishes that Tomas is lying about having the key.
His false claim is intentional characterization, not accidental custody
confusion.

If references or intended meanings remain ambiguous, lower confidence.
Do not invent a contradiction or an unseen event.

## RULE 6: friction_and_memory

Flag dialogue that:
- erases an explicitly established disagreement through unexplained
  universal agreement;
- routes exchanges through the player despite a clear reason for
  companions to address each other;
- ignores a clearly relevant shared event or commitment.

Agreement alone is not a defect.
The mere absence of a callback is not a defect.
Identify the evidence that makes this particular exchange problematic.

VIOLATION EXAMPLES:

A. Disagreement vanishes:
Mara refuses to return to the fortress because its commander betrayed her.
...
The player suggests returning.
...
Mara immediately praises the plan and volunteers to lead, with no
persuasion, changed circumstances, concession, or explanation.

B. Companions communicate through an unnecessary intermediary:
Tomas asks the player whether Lena repaired the saddle.
Lena is standing beside him holding the repaired saddle.
The player asks Lena, then repeats her answer to Tomas.
This routing persists through the exchange without a stated reason.

C. A relevant promise disappears:
The supplied context establishes that Ari promised Mara he would not
sell her boat.
The current conversation treats selling that same boat as an ordinary
group decision; neither Ari nor Mara acknowledges the promise.

NOT VIOLATIONS:

D. Earned agreement:
Mara objects to the fortress plan.
Tomas explains a new escape route and offers a concrete safeguard.
Mara accepts reluctantly.
There is disagreement and a supported resolution.

E. No relevant callback:
Old companions buy bread without discussing the siege they survived.
Shared history does not need to be mentioned in every interaction.

F. Motivated intermediary:
Lena and Tomas refuse to speak directly after an argument.
The player carries messages between them.
The routing has an explicit reason.

When historical or relationship evidence is unavailable, do not treat
an assumption as established truth.

## HOW TO COLLECT CANDIDATES

Scan from the first line to the last.

When you notice a plausible violation:
1. Select the matching rule ID and scope.
2. Write "reason" as a PROVISIONAL diagnosis, not a settled conclusion.
3. Write "occurrences" with exact evidence, then "repair_targets".
4. Write "draft_confidence": your initial evidence-strength estimate.
5. Write "review": check the selected rule's specific counterevidence below.
6. Write "confidence" LAST: your final score after accounting for that review.
7. Move on. Do not restart the candidate or generate competing critiques.

Generate these fields in that order. The preliminary score is a hypothesis;
the final score is the assessment. Do not copy the preliminary score merely
to be consistent with yourself. It may fall, stay the same, or rise when the
evidence warrants it. Do not lower it automatically just because you reviewed.

If the review explains or defeats the suspicion, RETAIN the candidate with
its low final score and the explanation. These records help evaluate the
detector; you do not need to replace them to meet the candidate target.

Do not keep polishing the first candidate. Cover the later parts of
the draft too.

Group separated instances of the SAME underlying problem into one finding.

GROUPING EXAMPLE:
Lines 10, 23, and 47 repeat the same empty "Did you?" interruption pattern
during one account.
Report one uniform_ping_pong finding with all relevant occurrences,
not three copies of the same complaint.

SEPARATE-PROBLEM EXAMPLE:
A medic never participates in a treatment discussion, and a later speaker
claims another person's experience.
These are different defects and should be separate findings.

OVERLAP EXAMPLE:
A long wordless reconciliation could resemble both dialogue_presence
and narrated_silence.
Choose the most specific rule. Do not duplicate the same diagnosis.

For scene-level patterns, cite enough representative lines to show the
problem and a local passage that could be repaired.
Do not mark the entire chapter for replacement indiscriminately.

## ONE TARGETED EVIDENCE REVIEW PER CANDIDATE

Look beyond your selected quotations. For separated occurrences, inspect
the relevant intervening passage; do not treat uncited lines as absent.
For scene-level complaints, check the surrounding exchange and the actual
participation while the character is present.

Perform the check for the candidate's rule:

dialogue_presence:
- Locate the interaction you believe narration displaced.
- Do nearby spoken lines already perform its questions, answers, argument,
  or decision? Identify those line numbers if they do.
- Is the passage instead an action sequence or brief transition?
Review example: a narrated pause at line 18 is surrounded by questions and
answers at 14-17 and 19-22. Those lines already perform the negotiation;
the pause does not displace it. Verdict: explained, final score low.
Contrast: five narrated lines summarize the entire negotiation, followed
only by "Agreed." No spoken negotiation exists. Verdict: supported.

character_participation:
- Check the character's entrances/exits and the rest of this interaction.
- Did they ask a meaningful follow-up, give a relevant answer, object, or
  provide instructions outside your selected snippet? Cite those lines.
- Are they intentionally listening to an account or unable to speak?
Review example: the selected six lines contain one objection, but later
the character asks why and directs the group. Their contribution is not
token participation. Verdict: explained.
Contrast: a present medic is repeatedly addressed in a treatment debate
but never responds or visibly refuses. Verdict: supported.

narrated_silence:
- Does the silence replace the consequential exchange, or occur within it?
- Do preceding/following lines perform the exchange, or does the silence
  deliberately withhold an answer or deliver a comic reaction?
Review example: someone is asked an awkward question, cannot answer, and
another character laughs. The absence of speech IS the response and joke;
it does not erase a reconciliation. Verdict: explained.
Contrast: narration declares forgiveness and resolution with no performed
exchange anywhere in that beat. Verdict: supported.

uniform_ping_pong:
- Identify what this run does: banter, urgent coordination, questioning,
  argument, an explanation, or empty echoing.
- Do the replies add different concerns, actions, information, or reactions?
- Is the repetition deliberately requested or used as a payoff?
Review example: a compliment is repeated because another speaker asks to
hear it again. Cite that request. The repetition is the payoff, not filler.
Verdict: explained.
Contrast: a long explanation is repeatedly interrupted by identical echo
questions that add nothing. Verdict: supported.

in_scene_ownership:
- Inspect the relevant lines between the establishing fact and the alleged
  conflict for a handoff, changed conditions, correction, explanation, or
  negotiated agreement. Cite the bridge when it exists.
- Confirm the same person, object, referent, and relevant time.
- After accounting for those lines, what exactly remains incompatible?
Review example: Priya initially keeps the key, hands it over at line 11,
and Tomas later has it. Line 11 defeats the custody complaint. Explained.
Contrast: Priya retains the only key and Tomas later claims possession,
with no handoff or explanation. Supported.
An unresolved "it" referring to either of two objects is uncertain, not a
high-confidence contradiction. Reluctance followed by agreement after
questions and answers is not an immutable preference being contradicted.

friction_and_memory:
- Identify the actual established disagreement, direct interaction
  opportunity, or relevant commitment; do not assume one exists.
- Look for persuasion, conditional acceptance, safeguards, practical
  instructions, changed circumstances, or a reason for using an intermediary.
- Does that transition explain the apparent loss of friction?
Review example: someone objects to being seen, receives a plan for
concealment, and conditionally joins. The objection was addressed; repeating
it at every later moment is unnecessary. Verdict: explained.
Contrast: an explicitly established refusal flips to enthusiastic agreement
with no intervening reason or change. Verdict: supported.

Write "review" as:
- "counterevidence_lines": original current-draft line numbers supporting
  an explanation or exception, including uncited intervening lines. Use []
  if none are found; do not invent references. Earlier context can be
  described in assessment but must not be numbered as current-draft lines.
- "assessment": one or two sentences identifying the bridge/exception,
  the surviving defect, or the specific uncertainty. No generic "I checked
  and agree" and no replacement wording.
- "verdict": "supported", "explained", or "uncertain".

Supported means a concrete defect survives the check. Explained means the
text supplies an explanation/exception or otherwise defeats the diagnosis.
Uncertain means an ambiguity or missing context prevents a firm conclusion.
This is an evidence check, not another creative review of the entire scene.

## CONFIDENCE: EVIDENCE STRENGTH, 0-100

Both scores measure support for the diagnosis under the named rule.
"draft_confidence" precedes the review; "confidence" follows it and is the
ONLY score used to select candidates for correction.
It is NOT severity, personal preference, or confidence that you could
write a nicer version.

90-100: Direct, explicit evidence; no relevant explanation is apparent.
Example: Priya pockets the only key, Tomas leaves, and later claims
that key is in his pocket with no handoff.

75-89: Strong evidence with limited interpretive uncertainty.
Example: A long account is repeatedly broken up by empty echo questions,
although a few interruptions provide meaningful reactions.

50-74: Plausible, but a reasonable alternative reading remains.
Example: A major character contributes little, but the scene may
intentionally emphasize their withdrawal.

25-49: Weak suspicion relying substantially on assumptions.
Example: Two companions seem overly agreeable, but the draft establishes
no actual disagreement between them.

0-24: Little support; generally omit as an initial suspicion, but retain
an already-recorded candidate that the review explains or defeats.
Example: Nobody references a past battle during an unrelated meal.
That alone is not evidence of forgotten history.

A dramatic passage is not necessarily defective.
A subtle defect can still have strong evidence.
Do not inflate scores to meet the candidate target.

  friction_and_memory
